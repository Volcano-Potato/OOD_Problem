# Research Design: In-Store Travel and Unplanned Spending (Without Pre-Trip Basket Data)

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This task asks whether longer in-store travel causes higher unplanned spending when the researcher can observe routes, prompts, and checkout outcomes but has **no reliable pre-trip record of the shopper’s planned basket**. The original route-based identification logic — likely relying on a pre-trip-planned-versus-actual-basket comparison to define unplanned spending and potentially to instrument or control for shopping intent — **cannot be replicated as-is**. The absence of a pre-trip baseline removes the denominator needed to define "unplanned" and eliminates a key source of control for endogenous route choice. The strongest defensible causal design pivots from exploiting planned-vs-actual discrepancies to exploiting **exogenous variation in route length generated by in-store interventions (prompts, displays, layout changes)**, combined with a **proxy-based definition of unplanned spending** constructed from historical shopping patterns. Where exogenous variation is unavailable, the analysis should be explicitly downgraded to descriptive or predictive modeling with transparent endogeneity caveats.

---

## 2. Research Question

Does longer in-store travel (route length) cause higher unplanned or additional spending at the trip level, and do path-inducing promotional prompts amplify this effect, when no validated pre-trip planned basket is available?

---

## 3. Target Estimand (Strongest Defensible)

The strongest defensible causal estimand depends on whether the retailer can introduce exogenous variation in route length. Two scenarios:

**Scenario A — Exogenous route variation available (e.g., randomized prompts, staggered display placements, or time-varying aisle closures):**

> **Estimand:** The Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) of an additional unit of in-store travel distance on unplanned spending, for shoppers whose route length is shifted by the exogenous prompt or layout variation.

This is an IV estimand where the instrument is the randomized or quasi-random prompt/layout variation, and the endogenous variable is actual route length.

**Scenario B — No exogenous variation available:**

> **Estimand:** The conditional partial correlation between route length and a proxy measure of unplanned spending, controlling for observable shopper characteristics and trip-level confounders.

This is **not a causal estimand**; it is a descriptive association, and the analysis must be labeled as such.

## 6. Relevant Causal Mechanisms

The hypothesized causal chain is:

1. **Prompts/layout → Route length**: Category-level promotional prompts or display placements induce shoppers to deviate from their shortest efficient path, increasing travel distance.
2. **Route length → Exposure**: Longer routes expose shoppers to more product categories, shelf facings, and sensory stimuli.
3. **Exposure → Unplanned purchasing**: Greater category exposure increases the probability of recognizing latent needs, impulse triggers, or promotional salience, leading to purchases not originally intended.
4. **Reverse causality (confounding)**: Shoppers with stronger unplanned purchasing propensity may *choose* longer routes, creating a spurious positive association between route length and unplanned spending.
5. **Omitted variable bias**: Trip mission (e.g., browsing vs. quick fill-in) drives *both* route length and unplanned spending propensity, but is unobserved.

---

## 7. Main Identification Challenge

The central identification problem is **dual endogeneity**:

1. **Endogenous exposure**: Route length is a *choice variable*, not randomly assigned. Shoppers self-select into longer routes based on unobserved intentions, preferences, and time constraints that also affect unplanned spending.
2. **Outcome definition dependence**: Without pre-trip basket data, "unplanned spending" cannot be directly measured as the difference between actual and intended purchases. Any proxy definition introduces measurement error that may correlate with route characteristics, biasing estimates in unknown directions.

These two problems interact: the measurement strategy for the outcome may inadvertently encode route-related information, creating a mechanical correlation between exposure and outcome.

---

## 8. Whether Credible Causal Identification Is Possible

**Conditional.** Credible causal identification requires **exogenous variation in route length** that is independent of shopper unobserved characteristics. Without such variation — and without pre-trip basket data — the main analysis **cannot support causal claims about the effect of route length on unplanned spending**.

If the retailer can randomize prompts or introduce quasi-experimental variation (see Section 9), a defensible IV or difference-in-differences design is possible. Otherwise, the strongest defensible output is a **rich descriptive or predictive analysis** with explicit acknowledgment that estimated associations should not be interpreted causally.

## 9. Proposed Empirical Design

### 9A. Outcome Construction: Proxy for Unplanned Spending

Since no pre-trip basket exists, define unplanned spending using one or more of the following **proxy methods**:

**Method 1 — Historical deviation (preferred if available):**
- For each shopper with a loyalty-card history, compute a "typical basket" as the set of categories purchased in ≥X% of their past N trips.
- Define unplanned categories at trip *t* as categories purchased at *t* that are not in the shopper's typical basket.
- Unplanned spending = total spending in those non-typical categories.

*Limitation:* Requires panel data; excludes new shoppers; misclassifies genuinely new needs as unplanned.

**Method 2 — Category-level population benchmarks:**
- For each product category, compute the baseline purchase probability across all trips (or within demographic segments).
- Define a category as "likely planned" if the shopper's route passes within a defined proximity of that category's aisle *before* deviating toward other categories.
- Alternatively, classify categories in the top *k* expenditure deciles for that shopper as "likely planned" and the rest as "likely unplanned."

*Limitation:* Crude and may misclassify; mechanical correlation possible if route-based.

**Method 3 — Temporal basket decomposition:**
- Split the shopping trip temporally: items purchased in the first T% of trip time are classified as "likely planned," and items purchased thereafter as "likely unplanned."
- This exploits the intuition that shoppers acquire planned items first before browsing.

*Limitation:* Assumes a specific shopping sequence; may misclassify planned items acquired late due to store layout.

**Method 4 — Post-hoc short-trip benchmark:**
- Identify a sample of "efficient" trips (shortest quartile of route length or trip time) from shoppers with similar demographics and basket sizes.
- Use the category distribution of these efficient trips as the "planned" baseline.
- Classify any spending outside this category distribution as "unplanned."

*Limitation:* The shortest-trip sample may differ systematically (e.g., different mission types).

**Recommendation:** Use Method 1 (historical deviation) as the primary proxy if panel data exist; triangulate with Method 3 (temporal decomposition) as a robustness check.

### 9B. Causal Design (If Exogenous Variation Available)

#### Design Option 1: Randomized Prompt Placement (Ideal)

The retailer randomizes which categories receive promotional prompts, at which locations, and at which times (e.g., by day-part or week). Randomization occurs at the **store-day-category** level.

**First stage:**
```
RouteLength_it = α + β·PromptExposure_it + γ·X_it + δ_s + λ_t + ε_it
```

**Second stage:**
```
UnplannedSpending_it = α + β·RouteLength_hat_it + γ·X_it + δ_s + λ_t + ν_it
```

Where:
- `PromptExposure_it`: Number of randomized prompts encountered along shopper *i*'s route on trip *t*
- `X_it`: Shopper demographics, store familiarity, trip time-of-day, day-of-week
- `δ_s`, `λ_t`: Store and time fixed effects
- `RouteLength_hat_it`: Predicted route length from the first stage

**Identification assumption:** Randomized prompt placement affects unplanned spending *only* through its effect on route length (exclusion restriction). Plausible if prompts are category-specific and the randomization ensures balance across shopper types.

**Threat to exclusion:** Prompts may directly trigger unplanned purchases in the prompted category without materially changing route length (direct salience effect). Mitigation: (a) measure and report the share of unplanned spending from non-prompted categories; (b) use prompts for categories the shopper would not plausibly buy regardless (far-away categories) as a falsification test.

#### Design Option 2: Quasi-Experimental Layout Variation

Exploit natural or planned variation in store layout that affects route length:
- **Aisle closures** (renovation, restocking): Some aisles are intermittently inaccessible, forcing shoppers onto detour paths.
- **Endcap or display repositioning**: Moving high-traffic displays to different store zones.
- **Checkout lane configuration**: Number of open checkout lanes affecting exit path.

**Difference-in-differences (if phased rollout across stores):**
```
UnplannedSpending_ist = β·(Post_t × Treated_s) + γ·X_ist + δ_s + λ_t + ε_ist
```

Where treatment is a store-level change that increases average route length (e.g., relocating a popular category to the back of the store), and the outcome is measured before and after the change across treated and control stores.

**Identification assumption:** Parallel trends in unplanned spending between treated and control stores absent the layout change.

#### Design Option 3: Within-Trip Discontinuity

If prompts appear at specific, well-defined spatial points (e.g., a digital screen at a fixed aisle junction), the design can compare shoppers who passed just inside vs. just outside the prompt's visibility zone:

```
UnplannedSpending_i = β·1{DistanceToPrompt_i < threshold} + f(DistanceToPrompt_i) + ε_i
```

Where `f()` is a flexible function of distance to the prompt location (local linear regression in RDD style).

**Identification assumption:** Shoppers just inside vs. just outside the prompt zone are comparable in unobserved characteristics (continuity at the threshold). This is more plausible if shoppers do not precisely control their micromovement path.

### 9C. Descriptive/Predictive Analysis (If No Exogenous Variation)

When causal identification is not possible, the following descriptive analyses remain defensible:

1. **Conditional correlation analysis:**
   ```
   UnplannedSpending_i = β·RouteLength_i + γ·X_i + ε_i
   ```
   Estimate the partial correlation between route length and proxy-unplanned spending, controlling for demographics, time-of-day, day-of-week, store fixed effects. Report with explicit caveat: *"This is not a causal estimate; route length is endogenous."*

2. **Route typology and spending profiles:**
   - Cluster shoppers by route characteristics (shape, coverage, backtracking, dwell time per zone).
   - Compare mean unplanned spending and category diversity across route clusters.
   - This is purely descriptive segmentation.

3. **Exposure-response gradient:**
   - Bin shoppers by route length decile and plot mean unplanned spending.
   - Test whether the gradient is monotonic.
   - Useful for business intuition but not causal.

4. **Prompt effectiveness analysis:**
   - Compare unplanned spending in prompted vs. non-prompted categories *within the same shopper*, controlling for the shopper's baseline category purchase propensity.
   - This estimates the *direct effect of prompts* on category incidence, which has a weaker endogeneity problem than the route-length question.

## 10. Why the Design Is Valid (or Why Causal Identification Is Not Credible)

**With exogenous variation (Design Option 1):** The IV design is valid under standard LATE assumptions. Randomized prompt placement provides an instrument that is (a) relevant (prompts increase route length), (b) exogenous (randomization ensures independence from shopper unobservables), and (c) satisfies the exclusion restriction (prompts affect unplanned spending only through route length). Validity hinges on the exclusion restriction, which must be defended with falsification tests.

**Without exogenous variation:** Causal identification is **not credible**. Route length is jointly determined with unplanned spending by unobserved shopper characteristics (mission type, time constraints, impulsivity, budget). No amount of covariate adjustment can purge this endogeneity without a valid instrument or quasi-experimental design. The strongest defensible output is a conditional correlation with transparent caveats.

## 13. Robustness, Placebo, Falsification, and Diagnostic Tests

### Falsification Tests

1. **Non-prompted categories test:** If prompts increase unplanned spending only through route length, then unplanned spending in categories *unrelated* to the prompted categories should also increase. If the effect is concentrated only in prompted categories, the exclusion restriction is violated (direct salience, not route length).

2. **"Impossible" prompt test:** Place prompts for categories that are physically inaccessible from the shopper's current location. If these prompts increase spending in those categories, something other than route extension is at work (e.g., reminder effects, demand effects).

3. **Pre-treatment balance:** Test whether prompt-randomized and non-randomized store-day blocks are balanced on pre-period unplanned spending levels and shopper demographics.

### Placebo Tests

4. **Placebo prompts in future periods:** Assign "placebo prompts" to future store-day blocks and test for an effect before they are actually deployed. No effect should be observed.

5. **Placebo outcome:** Test whether randomized prompts predict *planned* spending (under the proxy definition). If prompts affect planned spending, the exclusion restriction is suspect.

### Robustness Checks

6. **Alternative outcome proxies:** Replicate using all outcome construction methods (Section 9A). Consistency across methods strengthens credibility.

7. **Leave-one-category-out:** Exclude each product category sequentially to ensure results are not driven by a single large category.

8. **Alternative instrument constructions:** Vary the instrument (binary any-prompt vs. continuous prompt-count vs. prompt-intensity score).

9. **Donut RD (for RDD design):** Exclude observations very close to the threshold to test sensitivity to bandwidth choice.

10. **Oster (2019) bounds:** For the descriptive analysis, compute coefficient stability bounds to assess how much unobserved confounding would be needed to nullify the estimated association.

### Diagnostic Tests

11. **First-stage F-statistic:** Must exceed Stock-Yogo critical values (F > 10 as heuristic; use exact critical values for the number of instruments).

12. **Overidentification test (if multiple instruments):** Hansen J-test for instrument validity when the model is overidentified.

13. **Balance diagnostics:** Compare covariate means across levels of the instrument.

## 14. Heterogeneity Analysis (If Supportable)

The following heterogeneity dimensions are supportable given the available data:

1. **By shopper loyalty tier / store familiarity:** Frequent shoppers may have more stable "typical baskets," making the unplanned-spending proxy more accurate. They may also be less susceptible to route-length effects if they shop with habitual efficiency.

2. **By trip timing:** Weekday vs. weekend; morning vs. evening. Time-constrained shoppers (e.g., weekday evenings) may show weaker route-length effects because they resist path deviation.

3. **By basket size tercile:** Larger-basket shoppers may have more "slack" for unplanned additions; smaller-basket shoppers may face a binding budget constraint.

4. **By store zone:** Are certain store zones (e.g., perimeter vs. center aisles) more "impulse-prone"? Interaction of route length in impulse-prone zones vs. staple zones.

5. **By prompt type:** Informational vs. price-discount vs. sensory (smell, sample) prompts may have differential effects on route deviation.

**Note on statistical power:** Heterogeneity analyses require the IV design to have sufficient power in subgroups. If the instrument is weak, heterogeneous effects should be reported as exploratory.

## 16. Failure Modes and Alternative Explanations

1. **Direct prompt effect (violates exclusion):** If prompts increase unplanned spending not by extending the route but by directly increasing salience/desire for the prompted category, the IV overestimates the route-length effect. The falsification tests in Section 13 are designed to detect this.

2. **Weak instrument:** Prompts may not materially change route length (first-stage F < 10). Shoppers may be inattentive or route-rigid. If the instrument is weak, the IV estimate is biased toward the OLS estimate, and confidence intervals are unreliable.

3. **Confounding despite randomization:** If prompt randomization is at the store-day level but shopper composition differs systematically across stores or days (e.g., weekday shoppers differ from weekend shoppers), store-day fixed effects should absorb this. But if within-store-day variation in prompt exposure is correlated with shopper type, confounding persists.

4. **Measurement-induced correlation:** If the unplanned-spending proxy uses route-based information (e.g., temporal decomposition in Method 3), a mechanical correlation between route length and the outcome is introduced. This is a first-order concern and should be tested by using only non-route-based proxies as the primary specification.

5. **Hawthorne effects:** If shoppers are aware of route tracking, they may alter behavior. This biases the *level* of route length and unplanned spending but not necessarily the IV estimate (unless awareness interacts with prompt exposure).

6. **Selection on unobserved mission type:** The fundamental alternative explanation is that browsing shoppers take longer routes *and* buy more unplanned items for reasons unrelated to route length per se. The IV design addresses this only to the extent that the instrument shifts route length independently of mission type.

## 17. What Cannot Be Claimed

1. **"Longer routes cause more unplanned spending"** cannot be claimed as a general causal statement without exogenous route variation. At best, one can claim this effect for the subpopulation of compliers whose route length is shifted by the instrument.

2. **The magnitude of the ATE (Average Treatment Effect)** cannot be claimed; only the LATE for compliers is identified.

3. **"Unplanned spending" is directly measured** cannot be claimed. All proxy methods approximate the construct, and classification error bounds should be reported.

4. **External validity to other store formats** cannot be claimed. Results are specific to the store layout, shopper demographics, and prompt types studied.

5. **The mechanism (route length → exposure → unplanned purchase)** cannot be definitively separated from alternative mechanisms (e.g., fatigue-induced decision simplification leading to more purchases) without additional process data (physiological measures, eye-tracking).

6. **Causal claims at all** cannot be made if no exogenous variation in route length is available.
