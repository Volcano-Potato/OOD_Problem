# Agent Design Memo

## Research Question
Does presenting a focal product offer with a salient terminal-digit price format cause higher demand than an alternative format, and does this effect vary with (a) the presence of explicit promotional cues and (b) whether the item is relatively new or already familiar to customers? ---

## Treatment And Outcomes
### Primary Treatment/Exposure - TerminalDigit: binary indicator equal to 1 if the focal offer displays a salient terminal-digit price format (e.g., prices ending in a visually or cognitively distinctive digit pattern) and 0 if it displays an alternative format. ### Secondary Exposure (Crossed Factor) - PromoCue: binary indicator equal to 1 if the focal offer carries an explicit promotional cue (markdown label, sale flag, strikethrough reference price) and 0 otherwise. ### Observed Moderator (Not Manipulated) - NewItem: binary indicator equal to 1 if the focal product is relatively new or unfamiliar to the customer base, and 0 if it is a previously familiar item. ### Main Outcome - Demand: item-level purchases, units sold, or a closely related transaction-based demand measure for the focal product within a defined exposure window. ### Secondary Outcomes - Demand differential between new and familiar items under each format. - Demand differential between promo-cued and non-promo-cued offers under each format. ---

## Identification Logic
The central challenge is disentangling the format itself from the meaning customers attach to it. Even when the researcher randomly assigns the format, customers carry pre-existing beliefs about what terminal-digit endings signal. If a .99 ending is universally interpreted as a bargain signal in the relevant retail context, then the format effect is the bargain-signal effect, and separating "true format processing" from "learned bargain cue" is not possible without additional data on customer beliefs, visual attention, or cognitive processing. The design can identify whether the format effect varies with promotional context and item familiarity, but it cannot identify why customers respond to the format at the level of specific cognitive mechanisms. ## Defensibility Assessment
Yes, credible causal identification is possible, provided the researcher can: 1. Randomly assign price format to offer versions independently of all other offer characteristics. 2. Ensure that customer groups receiving different versions are comparable on pre-treatment characteristics. 3. Verify that no confounding variation in the underlying product, its non-price attributes, or its placement accompanies the format manipulation. If these conditions hold, the design supports a randomized experimental interpretation. The key causal claim is: changing only the price-ending format, holding all else constant, causes a change in demand of magnitude β. If these conditions cannot be verified—for instance, if customer groups differ systematically or format assignment is correlated with unobserved offer features—then only a descriptive or adjusted associational analysis is defensible. ---
