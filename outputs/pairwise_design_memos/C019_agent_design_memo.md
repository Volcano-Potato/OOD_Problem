# Agent Design Memo

## Research Question
Does increasing the distance a shopper travels through a physical retail store cause an increase in unplanned spending? A secondary question is whether path-inducing promotions — offers that intentionally redirect shoppers toward categories off their planned route — can increase unplanned purchases, and how the distance of the promotion from the planned route moderates this effect. ---

## Identification Logic
The core identification problem is that route length $R_i$ is endogenously determined within the shopping trip by the same factors that drive unplanned spending. Formally: $$Y_i = \beta R_i + X_i\gamma + \varepsilon_i$$ $$R_i = X_i\delta + \eta_i$$ where $Cov(\varepsilon_i, \eta_i) \neq 0$ because unobserved trip-level factors (impulsivity, time pressure, store familiarity, actual mission complexity beyond what is recorded) drive both route length and unplanned spending. Moreover, $Y_i$ can also cause $R_i$ (reverse causality), so even an instrument that shifts $R_i$ exogenously must be timed to precede the shopping trip to avoid contaminating the exclusion restriction. The pre-trip planned-basket data provide rich controls for Channel B (mission selection), but they do not resolve Channels C and D. ## Defensibility Assessment
Credible causal identification of the effect of route length on unplanned spending is possible only under an experimental design where additional travel distance is exogenously induced. Without experimental variation, the richest possible analysis is descriptive: decomposing the observed association using pre-trip covariates to bound the selection component, but never recovering a clean causal effect of route length itself. For the secondary question on path-inducing promotions, credible causal identification is possible through randomized promotion assignment, yielding the ITT effect of promotion assignment on unplanned spending. This is a different estimand (effect of a promotional intervention, not effect of route length per se) but addresses the retailer's operational question. ---

## What Cannot Be Claimed
1. The descriptive regression coefficient $\hat{\beta}$ on observed route length is not a causal effect. Even with rich pre-trip controls, it reflects a mixture of exposure, selection, and reverse causality. 2. The route-length IV estimate cannot be interpreted as the pure causal effect of travel distance. It captures the effect of an additional meter of induced travel specific to the promotion mechanism, not the effect of any additional meter of travel through any means. 3. The promotion ITT does not separate the effect of additional travel from the effect of exposure to the promoted category itself. The ITT is an estimate of a bundled intervention, not of route length in isolation. 4. Results from one store format or geographic market do not generalize to all retail settings.
