# Agent Design Memo

## Research Question
Do monitoring interventions — specifically (a) formal oversight mechanisms and (b) community-based monitoring — reduce corruption or resource leakage in local public projects, and through what channels do these two approaches operate? When official administrative reports may be manipulable, what outcome measures are credible, and how do the two monitoring types compare in reducing the discrepancy between independently measured project inputs/outputs and officially reported expenditures? ---

## Target Estimand

Normalizing by the planned budget (a pre-treatment baseline quantity) avoids the endogeneity of reported expenditure in the denominator. Secondary estimand: The ATE on independently assessed project quality or input-use measures, to capture leakage that manifests as quality-shading rather than pure quantity shortfalls. ## Identification Logic
The central challenge is that the outcome of interest (corruption/leakage) is not directly observed in administrative data, and the administrative data that are observed may be endogenously manipulated in response to the treatment itself. Specifically: - If treatment reduces reported discrepancies without affecting actual discrepancies, a naive comparison of administrative records across treatment arms would overstate the intervention’s effect. - If treatment causes corrupt actors to hide diversion better (e.g., through more sophisticated accounting manipulation rather than less theft), the intervention may appear effective in administrative data while being ineffective or even counterproductive in reality. - The direction of bias in any comparison using administrative records alone is ambiguous: it depends on whether monitoring makes records more or less honest. ## Defensibility Assessment
Yes, credible causal identification is possible, conditional on two features that the data structure supports: 1. Random assignment of projects or communities to monitoring conditions before implementation is complete (Stage 2 in the data structure), which eliminates confounding from unobserved project or community characteristics. 2. Independent outcome measurement at Stage 4 that is not constructed from — and cannot be contaminated by — the same administrative records that the treatment may affect. The researcher collects physical evidence directly. If random assignment is not possible (e.g., only government-chosen variation in audit probability is available), then credible causal identification is not possible without strong additional assumptions. In that case, the design would fall back to a conditional descriptive analysis, with explicit caveats about selection bias. ---

## Proposed Design
### 9.1 Randomization Stratify projects by baseline covariates (budget category, project type, administrative region, planned implementation timeline) and randomly assign to treatment arms within strata. The stratification improves precision and ensures balance on observable dimensions that may predict leakage. Sample size considerations: Power calculations should be conducted using plausible effect sizes and intra-cluster correlations (if assignment is clustered). A minimum detectable effect of approximately 0.15–0.25 standard deviations of the leakage measure is a reasonable benchmark. ### 9.2 Treatment Implementation - Formal oversight arm: Before implementation begins, officials are informed (by letter, official communication, or public announcement) that their project faces an elevated probability of formal audit or inspection. The actual audit probability should be credibly increased (e.g., from a baseline rate to 2–3× baseline). The announcement is the treatment; actual audit occurrence is an endogenous intermediate outcome. - Community monitoring arm: Before or early in implementation, community members receive structured information about the project’s planned budget, timeline, and specifications. ## What Cannot Be Claimed
1. The study cannot claim to measure total corruption. The independent leakage measure captures the gap between physical outputs and reported expenditures. It does not capture: corruption in project selection or budgeting (before Stage 1); bribery that does not affect physical outputs; diversion that is perfectly concealed in both records and physical outputs; or corruption in dimensions not covered by the measurement protocol. 2. The study cannot claim that the intervention eliminates corruption, even if the point estimate on leakage is zero. Substitution into unmeasured dimensions is always possible. 3. Without explicit modeling of spillovers, the study cannot claim that estimated treatment effects generalize to a fully treated population.
