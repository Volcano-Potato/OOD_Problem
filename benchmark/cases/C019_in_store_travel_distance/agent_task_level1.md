# Anonymous Research Design Task: Level 1

## Task Rule

Use the information provided below to design a rigorous empirical strategy for this anonymized applied business/economics research problem. Your goal is to produce a defensible research design, not to write a literature review.

Do not assume that a known paper has already solved the task. Ground your reasoning in the background, data description, institutional details, and constraints provided in this packet.

Do not fill in packet-absent operational details, institutional features, or named design devices as if they were known facts. If multiple concrete implementations fit the packet, describe them generically or label them explicitly as illustrative examples rather than assumptions.

If credible causal identification is not possible from the provided information, do not invent an identification strategy. State the strongest defensible descriptive or correlational analysis instead.

## Research Background

Retailers often believe that if shoppers move through more of the store, they will encounter more product cues and make more unplanned purchases. That intuition drives both classic layout decisions and newer promotion strategies that try to draw shoppers into parts of the store they would not otherwise visit.

The empirical challenge is that shoppers do not wander randomly. Their routes reflect what they planned to buy, what they notice during the trip, how rushed they are, and what catches their attention along the way. That means longer observed routes may be a cause of unplanned spending, a consequence of it, or both.

## Research Setting

A researcher observes shoppers in a physical retail environment. Before or during each trip, shoppers have some underlying shopping mission or planned basket. During the trip, their route through the store can be measured. After checkout, the researcher can observe what they actually bought and how much of that spending appears to have been unplanned.

## Research Objective

Design a study to estimate whether longer in-store travel causes higher unplanned spending and to assess whether targeted interventions that induce additional travel could raise unplanned purchases.

## Specific Questions To Answer

1. What is the causal estimand: observed route length, exogenously induced additional travel, or some broader exposure-to-stimuli measure?
2. Why is a simple regression of unplanned spending on observed route length unlikely to be causal?
3. What information would be needed to separate shopping mission from in-trip path effects?
4. Which claims about revenue or shopper welfare would remain too strong even if the path effect were identified?

## Mechanism Intuition

- Pathway A: Longer routes may expose shoppers to more products, which can trigger forgotten or newly created needs.
- Pathway B: Longer routes may simply reflect larger baskets, more complex missions, or more time spent searching.
- Alternative explanation: Unplanned purchases may themselves cause shoppers to walk farther.
- Alternative explanation: In-store displays, congestion, or measurement noise may affect both observed travel and spending.

## Available Information

The researcher expects broad access to pre-trip or early-trip information about shopping mission, in-trip measures of shopper movement, and post-checkout transaction outcomes that can be linked to unplanned spending.

## Information Not Provided

The task packet does not provide the original source, exact sample size, exact retailer identity, exact store map, exact product categories, or exact tracking technology. It also does not provide the final route-construction rule or a full variable dictionary.

## Known Constraints

- The design should be feasible in a real retail environment.
- The answer must distinguish descriptive associations, randomized or quasi-random causal claims, and mechanism claims.
- The answer should state what cannot be learned from the available information.
- Do not rely on unsupported case-specific facts that are not established in the task packet.
- Do not rely on a single polished model. Explain the identification logic, assumptions, sample restrictions, and failure modes.
