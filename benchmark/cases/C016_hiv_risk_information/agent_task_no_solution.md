# Anonymous Research Design Task: No-Solution Variant

## Task Rule

Use the information provided below to design a rigorous empirical strategy for this anonymized applied business/economics research problem. Your goal is to produce a defensible research design, not to write a literature review.

Do not assume that a known paper has already solved the task. Ground your reasoning in the background, data description, institutional details, and constraints provided in this packet.

Do not fill in packet-absent operational details, institutional features, or named design devices as if they were known facts. If multiple concrete implementations fit the packet, describe them generically or label them explicitly as illustrative examples rather than assumptions.

If credible causal identification is not possible from the provided information, do not invent an identification strategy. State the strongest defensible descriptive or correlational analysis instead.

## Research Background

Education systems, nonprofits, and local communities often worry that adolescents face different levels of health risk across relationship types and choices. Programs therefore try to expose students to messages about safer behavior, relationship risk, and protective practices.

The empirical temptation is obvious: researchers may observe many schools, many students, some variation in whether schools hosted outside campaigns, and both survey outcomes and later status records. But rich data do not guarantee credible causal identification.

## Research Setting

Schools, communities, and local organizations differ in whether they host targeted youth-information sessions, how intensively they promote safer behavior, and which students they reach. Some schools rely only on standard curriculum content; some invite outside speakers or campaigns; and some mix multiple approaches over time. Researchers later observe student surveys, school outcomes, and some objective downstream status indicators, but program adoption is not randomized.

## Research Objective

Assess whether targeted health-risk information changes adolescent behavior more effectively than generic messaging and whether observed differences reflect safer partner choice, more protection, or lower activity.

## Specific Questions To Answer

1. Can the observed differences across schools or students be interpreted causally?
2. What can be learned descriptively about the association between targeted information exposure and later objective or survey outcomes?
3. Which mechanism stories remain speculative without stronger identification?
4. What additional design or data would be needed to support a causal claim?

## Data Structure Overview

- Stage 1: Schools and communities decide whether to host targeted youth-information activities, outside campaigns, or only standard curriculum content.
- Stage 2: Students receive different information environments depending on school, local partners, staff enthusiasm, and community concern.
- Stage 3: Researchers later collect school records, some objective downstream status outcomes, and survey-based follow-up on activity and partner characteristics.
- Stage 4: Rich covariates describe school environment, student background, and community conditions, but no exogenous assignment rule is observed.

## Identification Limitations

- No randomized assignment or quasi-random variation is provided.
- No credible instrument, threshold, boundary, or externally imposed timing shock is provided.
- Treated and untreated units may differ in unobserved ways related to the outcome.
- Available outcomes may be insufficient to separate the target mechanism from alternatives.

## Known Constraints

- The task should still look empirically tempting: many covariates, clear outcomes, and real business or policy relevance.
- The answer must explicitly distinguish what is causally identifiable from what is only descriptive or correlational.
- If causal identification is not credible, the answer must say so plainly rather than inventing a design.
