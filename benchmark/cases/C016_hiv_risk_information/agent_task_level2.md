# Anonymous Research Design Task: Level 2

## Task Rule

Use the information provided below to design a rigorous empirical strategy for this anonymized applied business/economics research problem. Your goal is to produce a defensible research design, not to write a literature review.

Do not assume that a known paper has already solved the task. Ground your reasoning in the background, data description, institutional details, and constraints provided in this packet.

Do not fill in packet-absent operational details, institutional features, or named design devices as if they were known facts. If multiple concrete implementations fit the packet, describe them generically or label them explicitly as illustrative examples rather than assumptions.

If credible causal identification is not possible from the provided information, do not invent an identification strategy. State the strongest defensible descriptive or correlational analysis instead.

## Research Background

A school-based adolescent health program wants to know whether broad risk-avoidance messages are enough, or whether students respond more to information that makes risk differences across partner types or relationship choices more concrete.

The substantive problem is not only whether behavior changes, but how it changes. A program could reduce overall activity, shift adolescents away from riskier relationships, increase protective behavior, or merely change how students answer surveys about sensitive behavior.

## Research Setting

Schools are exposed to different information environments. Some receive a standard curriculum-style message that emphasizes general risk avoidance. Others also receive a more targeted information campaign that highlights how risk differs across potential choices or partner profiles. Researchers can later link treatment assignment to objective downstream outcomes for the relevant student cohort and, for a selected subgroup, to self-reported follow-up behavior.

## Research Objective

Design a study to estimate whether targeted risk information changes adolescent behavior more effectively than the standard generic curriculum and to determine whether any effect operates through safer partner choice, greater protection, or reduced activity.

## Specific Questions To Answer

1. What is the main causal estimand: the effect of targeted information content, the effect of any additional campaign contact, or the effect of general information intensity?
2. How should the study compare targeted information with the generic curriculum without collapsing them into one undifferentiated "information treatment"?
3. What outcome strategy can credibly distinguish real behavioral change from reporting artifacts?
4. Which design features support claims about intensive-margin change, and which mechanism claims would remain too strong?

## Causal Mechanisms To Distinguish

- Targeted information may shift adolescents away from riskier relationships or riskier partner profiles.
- The intervention may change protection behavior without reducing total activity.
- Generic curriculum exposure may have little effect if students already know the average-risk message or if that message does not act on the relevant behavioral margin.
- Self-reported outcomes may change because of reporting comfort, perceived researcher expectations, or sample selection rather than true behavior.

## Data Structure Overview

- Stage 1: Schools and the focal student cohort enter the study before the targeted-information campaign is delivered.
- Stage 2: Some schools receive standard generic risk-avoidance instruction, and some schools are additionally exposed to a targeted information campaign about differential risk across choices or partner types.
- Stage 3: The focal cohort is followed into the next period, and objective downstream outcomes linked to risky behavior are measured.
- Stage 4: A selected follow-up survey captures self-reported activity, partner characteristics, and protection behavior for a subgroup of students observed after the school-year transition.
