# Anonymous Research Design Task: No-Solution Variant

## Task Rule

Use the information provided below to design a rigorous empirical strategy for this anonymized applied business/economics research problem. Your goal is to produce a defensible research design, not to write a literature review.

Do not assume that a known paper has already solved the task. Ground your reasoning in the background, data description, institutional details, and constraints provided in this packet.

Do not fill in packet-absent operational details, institutional features, or named design devices as if they were known facts. If multiple concrete implementations fit the packet, describe them generically or label them explicitly as illustrative examples rather than assumptions.

If credible causal identification is not possible from the provided information, do not invent an identification strategy. State the strongest defensible descriptive or correlational analysis instead.

## Research Background

Small producers underuse a seasonal agricultural input even though modest use appears privately profitable. Researchers and policymakers want to know whether this reflects present-bias, liquidity pressure, simple price sensitivity, or ordinary heterogeneity in producer conditions.

The business and policy stakes are substantial, but a rich seasonal panel alone does not guarantee credible mechanism identification.

## Research Setting

The researcher observes repeated seasonal data on producers' input purchases, crop outcomes, prices faced at nearby vendors, self-reported cash-on-hand, and past adoption history. Some vendors or local programs offer promotions or discounts at different moments in the season, but those offers are not randomly assigned and often respond to local demand conditions or operational convenience.

## Research Objective

Assess whether earlier access to cheaper or easier input acquisition is associated with higher seasonal adoption and whether this can be interpreted as evidence of procrastination or present-bias.

## Specific Questions To Answer

1. Are earlier or cheaper opportunities associated with higher input adoption?
2. Can observed price and timing variation identify a procrastination or present-bias mechanism?
3. What can the data support descriptively or predictively about who adopts?
4. What additional design or data would be needed to support a causal mechanism claim?

## Data Structure Overview

- Stage 1: The researcher observes producers' prior adoption history, self-reported intentions, and seasonal resources.
- Stage 2: Prices, promotions, and ordering opportunities vary across locations and over time for operational reasons.
- Stage 3: Producers decide whether and when to buy the input before the seasonal application deadline.
- Stage 4: The researcher records eventual purchase, reported use, and downstream production outcomes.

## Variable Groups

### Treatment Or Exposure Variables

- Realized purchase timing.
- Observed local price or promotion.
- Acquisition-hassle measures.

### Selection Or Sample-Flow Variables

- Whether the producer visits vendors or markets.
- Whether the producer reports plans to buy.
- Whether the producer appears in follow-up data.

### Main Outcome Variables

- Actual seasonal input purchase.
- Actual seasonal input use.

### Secondary Outcome Variables

- Self-reported intention.
- Crop or production outcomes.
- Timing of purchase.

### Baseline Controls And Design Variables

- Prior adoption history.
- Household or farm characteristics.
- Self-reported liquidity and local market conditions.

## Identification Limitations

- No randomized assignment or quasi-random variation is provided.
- No credible instrument, threshold, boundary, or externally imposed timing shock is provided.
- Treated and untreated units may differ in unobserved ways related to both timing of purchase and adoption.
- Available outcomes are insufficient to separate procrastination or present-bias from alternative explanations using the observed variation alone.

## Known Constraints

- The task should still look empirically tempting: many covariates, clear outcomes, and real business or policy relevance.
- The answer must explicitly distinguish what is causally identifiable from what is only descriptive or correlational.
- If causal identification is not credible, the answer must say so plainly rather than inventing a design.
